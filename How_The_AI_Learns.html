1. What is Machine Learning?

Machine Learning (ML) is simply teaching a computer to make decisions by showing it lots of examples, instead of writing step-by-step rules for every situation.

Think of it like learning to cook:

Imagine you want to learn how much salt to add to different dishes. You could read a book of exact rules for every single food (that's normal programming). OR — you could taste 50 different dishes, note how much salt each one had, and eventually you just know how much salt a new dish needs. That's machine learning!

The computer "tastes" hundreds of examples and finds the pattern on its own.

Just like a chef learns from tasting many dishes, our AI learns from many sensor readings

How This Applies to Our Garden

Our hydroponic garden needs to decide: "How long should I run the pump to add nutrients?"

Instead of writing complicated rules like "if temperature is above 25 AND pH is below 6.5 AND nutrients are between 200-300, then run pump for exactly 4.7 seconds" (which would be impossible for every combination) — we let the AI learn the pattern from examples.

Key Idea

We show the AI many examples of: "When the garden looked like THIS → a human expert said the pump should run for THIS MANY seconds." After seeing enough examples, the AI figures out the pattern and can decide on its own for new situations.

The Three Steps of ML in Our Project

1

Collect Examples

A human looks at the sensors and records: "For these conditions, the pump should run for this long." This is our training data.

2

Train the AI

The computer studies all the examples and finds the hidden pattern — the relationship between sensor readings and dosing time.

3

Make Predictions

Now the AI can look at brand new sensor readings and predict the correct dosing time — even for situations it has never seen before!

Think of it like a study guide for an exam:

Imagine a teacher gives you a sheet with 200 practice problems AND their correct answers. You study the pattern: "Oh, when the question looks like this, the answer is usually around 5. When it looks like that, the answer is closer to 12." The CSV file is exactly this — a study guide with problems (sensor readings) and answers (dosing times) for the AI.

3. Method 1: Linear Regression — Drawing the Best Line

The simplest way our AI can learn is called Linear Regression. Don't let the fancy name scare you — it's really just drawing the best straight line through a cloud of dots.

Imagine you're plotting grades vs study hours on graph paper:

You ask 30 classmates: "How many hours did you study?" and "What grade did you get?" You plot each person as a dot. Then you take a ruler and draw the one straight line that passes as close as possible to ALL the dots. Now, if a new student says "I studied 5 hours," you can use your line to predict their grade. That's Linear Regression!

The purple line is the "best fit" — it gets as close to all the green dots as possible

How It Works in Our Garden

The computer draws a "line" (actually a formula) that connects sensor readings to dosing time. The formula looks like this:

Dosing Time = starting point
+ (weight₁ × temperature)
+ (weight₂ × humidity)
+ (weight₃ × pH)
+ (weight₄ × nutrients)

The "weights" are just numbers that tell the AI how important each sensor is and in which direction it pushes the dosing time.

Think of the weights like volume knobs on a music mixer:

Each sensor gets its own "volume knob." Training is the process of turning each knob up or down until the output sounds right (matches the correct answers). A weight of +3.5 means "this sensor pushes dosing time UP a lot." A weight of -1.2 means "this sensor pushes dosing time DOWN a little."

What "Training" Actually Does

Training is the computer trying millions of different weight combinations until it finds the set of weights that gives the smallest total error across ALL examples. It's like a music producer tweaking knobs until the song sounds perfect.

In Plain English

Linear Regression says: "I believe the answer is a simple recipe — just multiply each sensor reading by some number, add them up, and that's my prediction." The computer finds the best numbers to multiply by.

Limitation

A straight line can only capture simple, proportional relationships. If the real pattern is curved or complex (e.g., pH matters way more when nutrients are also low), Linear Regression can't capture that. For complex patterns, we need the Neural Network — explained next!

4. Method 2: Neural Network (MLP) — A Mini Brain

The second method is called a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) — a type of neural network. Think of it as a smarter, more flexible version of the line-drawing method.

Imagine a team of 8 advisors helping a manager make a decision:

The manager needs to decide how long to run the pump. Instead of looking at the sensors directly, they ask 8 expert advisors. Each advisor looks at ALL four sensors, thinks about it in their own unique way, and gives a recommendation. The manager then listens to all 8 advisors and combines their opinions into one final answer.

Some advisors are "temperature experts," some care more about pH — each one weighs the inputs differently. Together, they can understand complex patterns that no single person could catch alone.

Our neural network: 4 inputs → 8 "advisors" (hidden neurons) → 1 output (dosing time)

The Three Layers Explained

IN

Input Layer — "The Questions"

The 4 sensor readings come in: Temperature, Humidity, pH, and Nutrient Level. These are like the "exam questions" being passed to the advisors. Each value is scaled to be between 0 and 1 (so they're all on the same playing field).

H

Hidden Layer — "The 8 Advisors" (This is where the magic happens!)

Each of the 8 neurons (advisors) receives ALL 4 inputs, multiplies each by its own personal weight, adds them up, and decides: "Should I activate or stay quiet?" (This yes/no decision is called ReLU — if the sum is negative, the advisor says nothing. If positive, they pass their value forward.)

It's called "hidden" because you never see this layer directly — it works behind the scenes.

OUT

Output Layer — "The Final Decision"

The manager neuron listens to all 8 advisors, weighs their opinions, and produces one final number: the predicted dosing time in seconds.

Why Is This Better Than a Straight Line?

Think of it like directions to school:

Linear Regression can only give directions using one straight road: "Go north for 2km." But the Neural Network can give complex directions: "Go north, then turn left at the roundabout, take the third exit, then curve right." It can follow twists and turns in the data that a straight line would miss completely.

Fun Fact

Our neural network has 49 weights in total (8 advisors × 4 inputs + 8 biases + 8 output weights + 1 output bias = 49 numbers). During training, the computer adjusts all 49 knobs simultaneously until the predictions are as close to the correct answers as possible. The big AI models like ChatGPT have billions of these weights — ours is tiny but perfectly suited for our garden!

5. The Training Process — How the AI "Studies"

Training is the step where the computer actually learns from the data. Here's exactly what happens behind the scenes when you press "Execute Machine Learning Pipeline":

Imagine learning to throw darts blindfolded:

You throw a dart (guess an answer). A friend tells you "You were 3 inches too far left." You adjust and throw again. "Now you're 1 inch too high." You adjust again. After hundreds of throws with feedback, you're hitting near the bullseye almost every time — even blindfolded. This is exactly how the AI trains: guess, check the error, adjust, repeat.

Step-by-Step: What the Computer Does

1

Load the Examples

The computer reads all 200 rows from the CSV file. Each row becomes one practice problem. The sensor readings are the "question" and the dosing time is the "correct answer."

2

Normalize the Numbers (Put Everyone on the Same Scale)

Temperature goes up to 50, but humidity goes up to 100, and nutrients go up to 1023. That's unfair — bigger numbers would dominate smaller ones. So we shrink everything to a scale of 0 to 1 by dividing each value by its maximum.

Temperature 25°C → 25 ÷ 50 = 0.50    Humidity 68% → 68 ÷ 100 = 0.68

Now all inputs are on equal footing — like converting different currencies into one standard currency.

3

Start With Random Weights (Random Guesses)

The computer assigns random numbers to all the weights. At this point, its predictions are terrible — like a student guessing randomly on a test. That's okay — this is just the starting point.

4

Make Predictions and Measure the Error

The computer runs every example through the formula and compares its prediction to the correct answer. The difference is the error. It adds up all the errors across all 200 examples to get one big "total error score."

5

Adjust the Weights to Reduce Error (The Learning!)

The computer nudges each weight slightly — some up, some down — trying to make the total error smaller. It uses math (calculus) to figure out which direction each weight should move. This is repeated thousands of times (up to 5,000 rounds for our MLP).

6

Stop When It's Good Enough

Eventually, the errors get so small that more adjustments don't help much. The weights are now "learned" — they capture the pattern in the data. Training is done!

In Summary

Training is just: Guess → Check Error → Adjust → Repeat (thousands of times). Each round, the predictions get a little more accurate. By the end, the AI has "learned" the relationship between sensor readings and dosing times.

6. How Do We Know If the AI Learned Well?

After training, we need to check: "Did the AI actually learn, or is it still guessing?" We use two report card grades to measure this.

Grade 1: R² Score — "How Much of the Pattern Did It Capture?"

Think of R² like a percentage grade on a test:

An R² of 0.85 means the AI understood 85% of the pattern in the data. The remaining 15% is noise or patterns too complex for the model to catch. An R² of 1.0 would be a perfect 100% — the AI nailed every single prediction exactly.

0.20
Poor (20%)
The AI barely learned anything. It's almost just guessing randomly. Needs more data or a better model.
0.65
Decent (65%)
The AI learned the basic pattern but misses some nuances. Acceptable for simple tasks.
0.85
Good (85%)
The AI learned most of the pattern. Its predictions are usually close to correct. This is our target!
0.95
Excellent (95%)
The AI captured almost all the pattern. Predictions are very accurate and reliable.

Grade 2: MAE — "How Far Off Are the Predictions On Average?"

Think of MAE like the average number of points you miss on a quiz:

If the MAE is 1.2 seconds, it means the AI's predictions are off by about 1.2 seconds on average. So if the correct answer was "run the pump for 5.0 seconds," the AI might say 3.8 or 6.2 seconds. That's pretty close! If the MAE was 8.0 seconds, the AI would be wildly inaccurate.

1.0 — 2.5sPredictions are off by 1–2.5 seconds✅ Good — close enough for the garden
2.5 — 5.0sPredictions are off by a noticeable amount⚠️ Needs more data or better model

7. Deployment — Putting the AI Brain Into the Garden

After training, the AI's "knowledge" is just a small list of numbers — the weights it learned. We save these numbers in a tiny file and upload it to the garden's microcontroller.

Think of it like a cheat sheet:

After studying 200 practice problems, a student writes down the key formulas on a small index card. They don't need the textbook anymore — just the card with the important numbers. That index card is our model_params.json file — a tiny file containing only the learned weights.

What's Inside model_params.json?

It's just a text file with numbers. For the Linear Regression model, it looks like this:

// This is the AI's "cheat sheet" — just 5 numbers!
{
  "model_type": "linear",
  "w_dosing": [
    8.2,      ← starting point (if all sensors were zero)
    -1.5,     ← temperature weight (hotter → slightly less dosing)
    -0.3,     ← humidity weight (more humid → slightly less dosing)
    +2.8,     ← pH weight (higher pH → MORE dosing needed)
    -4.1      ← nutrient weight (more nutrients → LESS dosing)
  ]
}

That's it! The entire "intelligence" of a Linear Regression model is just 5 numbers. The MLP neural network has 49 numbers — still incredibly small.

How the ESP-01 Uses the Model

1

Upload the Weights

You drag-drop model_params.json onto the dashboard. The file is saved to the ESP-01's flash memory (like saving to a USB drive).

2

Switch to AI-Edge Mode

Click "AI-Edge" on the dashboard. Now the microcontroller runs the learned formula every 15 seconds using live sensor data.

3

Real-Time Predictions

The ESP-01 reads the current sensors, plugs them into the formula with the learned weights, and gets a prediction. If it says "dose for 4.2 seconds," it tells the Arduino to run the pump for exactly 4.2 seconds. No internet needed — it all runs locally on a chip smaller than a coin!

This is Called "Edge AI"

When an AI model runs directly on a tiny device (like our ESP-01) instead of on a big cloud server, it's called Edge AI. The "edge" means the outermost point of a network — the actual device in the field. Our garden doesn't need WiFi or the internet to make predictions. The trained brain lives right inside the garden itself!

The Self-Learning Loop

Here's the coolest part: our system can keep learning and improving itself! A background program on a laptop connected to the garden automatically:

  1. Downloads new calibration data from the garden every 10 seconds
  2. Retrains the AI model with the new data
  3. Only deploys the updated model if it's better than the previous one (higher R² score)
  4. The garden gets smarter over time — automatically, without human intervention!
The Big Picture

Data → Training → Weights → Deployment → Predictions → More Data → Retrain → Better Weights → Better Predictions. It's a virtuous cycle — the AI keeps getting smarter the more the garden runs.
